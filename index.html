An equipment management system, three plants

Lite-On Technology · Guangzhou · 2019–2022 · C#, ASP.NET MVC, Oracle

At Lite-On, machine maintenance ran on paper. You had to walk the floor and read paper forms to know which machines were down. Maintenance schedules were on paper too. So maintenance got missed, and there was no early warning.

The three plants made different products. Car lights, casings, and circuit boards. Different product lines, different processes.

I architected an Equipment Management System in C# and ASP.NET MVC. Two key design decisions.

One shared system for all three plants, on a single Oracle database, with a plant dimension separating the product lines. Why one system? We were a small team. One codebase means one set of fixes, not three. And one database means we could compare the lines and share the same reports.

Second, hybrid data collection. We connected machine signals directly where the equipment supported it. We used manual reporting for the rest. The system also pulled ERP data from SAP and combined it with the machine-status data.

We piloted at one plant first, then rolled out to the other two. We adjusted per plant, because the processes differed. And we trained the floor staff on the new workflow.

This year: delivery platform for a compliance engine

Carnegie Mellon capstone with IRALOGIX · 2026 · five-person team · AWS EKS, Helm, ArgoCD

A regulatory-compliance platform for retirement accounts. I took the delivery and platform side. Mine, end to end:

  • Packaged the API service as a Helm chart and delivered it to EKS through an ArgoCD Application.
  • Built the self-hosted GitHub Actions runner fleet with rootless BuildKit, so images build without privileged containers. I proved the GitOps loop rather than assuming it: scaled replicas to watch auto-sync, added and removed a ConfigMap to watch prune.
  • Implemented Ed25519-signed JWT verification for internal service calls. Routes by issuer, fails closed with no fallback path, 12 pytest tests.
  • Designed and implemented the observability architecture: the application emits OpenTelemetry only and a Collector selects the backend at deploy time; the backend changed three times in three weeks and the application did not.
  • CIDR-restricted ALB ingress, and the cutover off mock auth.

Not mine, since it was a team: the ArgoCD PreSync hook for schema migrations, the IRSA trust setup, and the ArgoCD bootstrap were teammates' work, as were the verdict engine, the admin CRUD and the OpenAPI schema. The EKS cluster and External Secrets Operator already existed. I installed onto that platform, I did not provision it.

Two internal tools from the same project

A source validator for regulatory rule models. For a given US state it maps every field of the rule-model schema to a confirmed official source URL, plus instructions for finding it on that page. Most of the design is about what it refuses to do: an explicit source-priority chain, unconfirmed fields emitting null and being flagged rather than guessed, and a human review gate before anything counts as final.

A meeting pipeline, Zoom through Drive to Confluence, packaged as agent skills over a Python package. A sync mode diffs local folders against Confluence by date prefix and publishes only what is missing. 1,866 lines. It manages the 102 pages carrying its labels, and I wrote 89 of them.

The pipeline contains no model call. It is deterministic automation authored as agent skills. The skills are the interface, not the intelligence, and the summary comes from Zoom.

The team's wider AI tooling, including its context hub and most of its agent skills, was built by teammates.
